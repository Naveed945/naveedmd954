# Artifact 3: Explaining Neural Networks in Plain Language

**Title:** Explaining Neural Networks in Plain Language — A Discussion Post and Knowledge Check

**Objective:** To demonstrate the ability to translate a complex AI/ML concept (how neural networks learn) into an explanation accessible to a non-technical audience, while also validating my grasp of the underlying technical concepts through a formal knowledge assessment.

**Process:** I started by identifying the core mechanics of a neural network — neurons, weights, layers, and the training loop — and then worked backward to find an everyday analogy (a child learning to recognize a dog) that captured the same logic without jargon. I built the explanation in stages: a relatable scenario first, then the technical mapping, then a connection to change leadership and how employees adapt to AI-driven change. Separately, I completed a 15-question knowledge check covering neural network architecture, training mechanics, and historical foundations, which tested whether my conceptual understanding held up against precise technical definitions. For this portfolio, I revised both pieces, adding a comparison table and structured reflection, and combined them into a single artifact to show both communication skill and technical accuracy side by side.

**Tools:** Markdown for formatting and publishing to GitHub Pages, course discussion boards and the LMS quiz tool for the original coursework, and GitHub for hosting and version control of the portfolio itself.

**Value Proposition:** This artifact shows that I can do more than pass a technical assessment — I can take that same knowledge and make it usable for people who don't have a technical background, like a manager or client who needs to understand what a model is doing without needing to understand the math. That combination of technical grounding and clear communication is something I'd want any employer evaluating my AI/ML skills to see.

## Part A: Plain-Language Explanation

*Original context: A discussion post responding to the prompt of explaining neural networks simply.*

Imagine you're teaching a young child to recognize a dog. You don't hand them a dictionary definition. Instead, you point at dozens of different dogs — big ones, small ones, fluffy ones, spotted ones — and say "dog" each time. Eventually, without ever memorizing a rulebook, the child just knows what a dog looks like, even a breed they've never seen before.

A neural network learns the same way.

Think of it as a network of tiny decision-makers, called "neurons," stacked in layers:

- The **input layer** looks at the raw information (like the pixels of a photo).
- The **hidden layers** in the middle each look for small patterns — one layer might notice edges and curves, the next might notice shapes like ears or a snout, and so on, building up in complexity.
- The **output layer** makes the final call: "dog" or "not a dog."

Here's the key part: at first, the network is terrible at this. It guesses randomly. But every time it's wrong, it goes back and slightly adjusts itself, like tightening or loosening thousands of tiny dials, so it's a little more accurate next time. Show it enough examples — thousands, sometimes millions — and it keeps tuning those dials until it gets remarkably good at telling dogs from cats, spam from real emails, or fraud from a real transaction.

That's really it. A neural network doesn't "think" the way we do. It's a pattern-matching system that keeps adjusting itself based on feedback until its guesses become reliable.

**Why this matters for change leadership:** just like a neural network needs repeated exposure and feedback to learn, employees adapting to AI-driven change need the same — repeated, digestible exposure to new concepts, real examples, and a feedback loop to check understanding, rather than a single info-dump.

*Tool disclosure: I used Claude (Anthropic) to help draft and refine this explanation, applying the chunking, storytelling, and progressive disclosure principles from this week's lesson.*

**Instructional design choices I applied:**
- **Chunking** — breaking the explanation into neuron → layers → training loop
- **Progressive disclosure** — leading with the dog-recognition example before any technical terms
- **Relevance and context** — tying the concept to change leadership and how employees adapt to AI-driven change
- **Storytelling** — using the child-learning-a-dog narrative to make an abstract process feel familiar

These choices weren't accidental. They draw directly on principles I learned in this course around communication in change leadership: breaking complex ideas into digestible pieces, adapting the message to the audience, and reinforcing key points so they move from short-term to long-term understanding. Explaining a neural network to a non-technical coworker is, in a small way, its own kind of change leadership — asking someone to trust and adopt a new, unfamiliar way of thinking about a problem. The same techniques that help a manager communicate organizational change (tailored messaging, storytelling, progressive disclosure) are what make a technical concept land with someone who has no reason to already believe it matters to them.

## Reflection

**Customization for the Audience**

My original discussion post was written for classmates who already had some background in AI/ML. For this portfolio, my intended audience is broader — potentially a hiring manager, recruiter, or client who may not have a technical background at all. To account for that, I kept the dog-recognition analogy front and center rather than treating it as a warm-up before the "real" content, and I moved technical vocabulary later in the explanation, only after the underlying concept was already clear. I also added the comparison table summarizing the quiz topics, since a reviewer skimming a portfolio is more likely to scan a table than read dense paragraphs of quiz content. Framing the two pieces together, rather than as separate, disconnected artifacts, was itself a customization decision: it tells a reviewer a complete story (technical competence plus communication skill) instead of leaving them to infer the connection.

**Lessons Learned**

Revising this artifact for a portfolio audience taught me that explaining something simply is a different skill from understanding it. I understood the mechanics of neural networks well enough to do well on the quiz, but figuring out how to represent that understanding without jargon took more effort than I expected. This connected directly to a separate lesson in the course on communication in change leadership, which reinforced that clear communication isn't just about simplifying content, it's about tailoring the message, sequencing information thoughtfully, and reinforcing key ideas so they actually stick with the audience. I also learned that a portfolio artifact often needs restructuring, not just repackaging. My original discussion post worked fine as a forum reply, but it wasn't built to stand alone as a portfolio piece. Adding a clear objective, process, and value proposition upfront forced me to articulate why this artifact matters, something I hadn't needed to do for the original assignment.

**Feedback and Revisions**

Peer replies to my original discussion post responded positively to the dog-recognition analogy and its connection to change leadership, which confirmed that the plain-language approach was landing the way I intended. That feedback is part of why I kept the analogy as the centerpiece of the portfolio version rather than trimming it down. I also revised the artifact after reviewing the portfolio rubric, which emphasizes tailoring content to different audiences; that pushed me to add the explicit reflection on audience customization above, since my first draft assumed the reasoning behind my choices would be obvious to a reviewer without being spelled out.
